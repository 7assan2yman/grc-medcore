How to Use This Pack
This document is your working resource for the MedCore Clinics GRC project. It is written the way a real organization's
design-phase documentation would look before a system goes live — that's intentional. In a real job, nobody hands a GRC
analyst a clean asset inventory or a list of risks. You build the asset inventory yourself by reading design and architecture
documentation, and you derive risks from what that documentation exposes (or fails to address).
There are three parts to this pack:
 1. Section 2 — The scenario and business context (why this project exists, who the stakeholders are, what "done" looks
    like).
 2. Section 3 — Design & Architecture documentation for the fictional organization (system design, data flows, network
    design, third parties, planned controls). Treat this exactly as you would a real design package handed to you before go-
    live.
 3. Section 4 — Your deliverables, with the methodology, templates, and scoring scales you must use, so both teams in this
    category are graded on the same basis.
Work only from what is written in Section 3 plus reasonable, clearly-labelled assumptions. If you assume something that
isn't stated, write it down as an assumption in your report — don't silently invent facts.


2. Scenario & Business Context
MedCore Clinics is a chain of 14 outpatient clinics operating across three cities. Historically each clinic kept patient records
in a locally-installed practice-management application with paper backups. Leadership has approved a project to migrate all
patient records to a single cloud-based Electronic Health Record (EHR) platform so that any clinic can pull up any patient's
history, appointments can be scheduled centrally, and billing can be consolidated. No security function currently exists at
MedCore beyond a shared IT helpdesk contractor; there is no security policy, no formal access control process, and no prior
risk assessment on file.
The project is in the design phase. Executive sponsors have approved the architecture described in Section 3 and asked the
(fictional) GRC function to deliver a risk assessment, a control gap analysis against a recognized framework, and a
prioritized remediation plan before any development or migration work begins.

Stakeholders
 • Clinical Directors (3) — accountable for patient care continuity; care about system uptime and clinician usability
 • Chief Operating Officer — project sponsor; accountable for the business case and go-live date
 • Billing & Revenue Cycle Manager — owns claims/insurance data flows
 • IT Helpdesk Contractor (3rd party, 4 staff) — currently the only technical resource; will operate the new platform post-
   launch
 • Patients — data subjects; no formal representation, but regulatory obligations exist on their behalf

Regulatory Context
MedCore treats patients in a jurisdiction where health data is subject to data-protection and health-privacy regulation (treat
this as broadly equivalent to HIPAA-style obligations for the purposes of this exercise: breach notification duties, minimum-
necessary access, and audit trail requirements for patient data). Assume these obligations apply and factor them into your
impact ratings.

4. Your Deliverables
You are acting as the GRC team for this organization during its design phase. Your job is to tell the organization what could
go wrong before it goes live, how bad each thing would be, and what to fix first — mapped to a recognized framework.
Everything is qualitative: no dollar-value loss modeling, no ALE/SLE calculations. Use descriptive scales
(Low/Medium/High/Critical) throughout.

4.1 Asset Inventory (build this first)
Before you can assess risk you need to know what you're protecting. Read Section 3 and extract every asset into a register.
An asset is anything of value: a system, a data store, an interface, a credential set, a piece of infrastructure, or a third party
with access. For each asset, capture:
 • Asset ID and name
 • Asset type (data / application / infrastructure / third party / process)
 • Description and owner (infer a reasonable owner if not stated, and label it as inferred)
 • Data classification or criticality (Public / Internal / Confidential / Restricted)
 • Where it sits in the architecture (which zone / network segment / environment)
Target: a minimum of 12 distinct assets, pulled directly from the design documentation, not invented.

4.2 Framework Mapping
Pick one recognized framework and use it consistently across the whole report: ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A, or NIST CSF
2.0. State which one you chose and why it fits this organization in one paragraph. Every gap and every risk in your report
must reference at least one control or function from that framework (e.g., "ISO 27001 A.8.24 – Use of cryptography" or
"NIST CSF PR.DS-01").

4.3 Gap Analysis
For each relevant control area in your chosen framework, assess the organization's design against it in three states: Not
Addressed / Partially Addressed / Fully Addressed. This is a design-phase gap analysis — you are checking whether the
design documentation shows evidence of intent to implement the control, not whether it's actually running yet. Build a table
with these columns:
 • Control reference (framework ID)
 • Control area / requirement
 • Current state per the design docs (Not / Partially / Fully Addressed)
 • Evidence or citation from Section 3 supporting your rating
 • Gap description — what's missing and why it matters
Minimum 15 control areas assessed.

4.4 Qualitative Risk Matrix (minimum 12 risks)
Use the standard 5x5 Likelihood x Impact matrix and scoring scale below for both GRC teams — this keeps scoring
consistent across teams so results are comparable.

 Score               Rating                    Design-Phase Definition

 1                   Rare                      Would require multiple independent failures or highly unusual
                                               conditions; no realistic path in this design.

 2                   Unlikely                  Possible but design includes some mitigating factor; would need an
                                               uncommon trigger.

 3                   Possible                  Plausible given how the design is described; comparable issues are
                                               common in similar systems.

 4                   Likely                    Design leaves an evident, exploitable gap; expected to occur without
                                               intervention.

 5                   Almost Certain            Design has no meaningful control against this; near-guaranteed
                                               given normal operating conditions.

 1                   Negligible                No material effect on operations, data, safety, or compliance.

 2                   Minor                     Localized, short-lived disruption; no sensitive data or safety

                                               within normal operations.

For each risk in your register, capture:
 • Risk ID
 • Risk description (threat + vulnerability + affected asset, one sentence)
 • Related framework control(s) from your gap analysis
 • Likelihood rating (1-5) with one-line justification

4.5 Business Impact Analysis (BIA) — qualitative
For the organization's 4-6 most critical business processes or systems (drawn from Section 3), assess:
 • Process/system name and description
 • Criticality tier (Mission-Critical / Important / Standard)
 • Impact of disruption across four dimensions, rated Low/Medium/High/Severe each: Financial, Operational,
   Reputational, Legal/Regulatory
 • Maximum Tolerable Downtime (MTD) — a qualitative estimate (e.g., "minutes", "hours", "1-2 days") with justification
 • Recovery Time Objective (RTO) and Recovery Point Objective (RPO) — qualitative estimates with justification
 • Dependencies (systems, vendors, or processes this relies on)

4.6 Prioritized Remediation Roadmap
Take your risk register and gap analysis and produce a prioritized action list an executive could actually approve. Group into
three horizons:
 • Before go-live (must-fix — blocks launch)
 • First 90 days post-launch
 • Longer-term / continuous improvement
For each item: what to do, which risk(s)/gap(s) it addresses, rough effort (Low/Medium/High), and owner (role, not a name).

4.7 Executive Summary & Presentation
A one-page executive summary written for a non-technical board: overall risk posture in plain language, top 3-5 risks, and
the single most important recommendation. Your team will also present a 10-12 minute walkthrough of your findings, in the
roles listed in your scenario card (Risk Analyst, Compliance Officer, Threat Modeler, Control Owner/Auditor, Policy Writer,
Team Lead/Presenter — or the OT-specific role set for Atlas).

5. Team Roles & Task Breakdown
Six roles are named on your scenario card. Each one below has a specific piece of the project to own — every role produces
something that ends up in the final report, and no role is a spectator.

Risk Analyst
 • Build and own the qualitative risk register (Section 4.4) — minimum 12 risks, each scored with the shared 5x5
   Likelihood x Impact scale.
 • Justify every Likelihood and Impact rating in one line, tied to something specific in the design documentation.
 • Recommend a treatment (Mitigate / Transfer / Accept / Avoid) for each risk.



Compliance Officer
 • Choose and justify the framework (ISO 27001 or NIST CSF) for the whole report.
 • Build the gap analysis table (Section 4.3) — minimum 15 control areas, each rated Not/Partially/Fully Addressed with
   evidence cited from the design docs.
 • Flag which gaps represent a regulatory exposure (breach notification, minimum-necessary access, audit trail) given
   MedCore's regulatory context.



Threat Modeler
 • Produce a short threat model of the ClinicCloud data flow: list realistic threat actors (e.g., malicious insider at a clinic,
   compromised helpdesk contractor, credential-stuffing attacker, careless vendor) and the attack path each could take
   through the architecture in Section 3.
 • Feed these attack paths into the Risk Analyst's register as the "threat" half of each risk description.

Team Lead / Presenter
 • Own the executive summary (Section 4.7) — one page, plain language, top 3-5 risks, single most important
   recommendation.
 • Integrate all sections into one coherent final report and check for consistency (same framework language, same risk IDs
   referenced consistently across sections).
 • Lead the 10-12 minute team presentation and coordinate who presents which section.




6. Ground Rules
 • Do not ask the instructor for the asset inventory — it is your job to extract it from Section 3.
 • Every risk and gap must cite something concrete from the design documentation (a section, a diagram element, a stated
   data flow).
 • If information isn't in the design docs, state your assumption clearly rather than guessing silently.
 • Stay qualitative throughout — no fabricated financial figures.
 • Use one framework consistently; do not mix ISO 27001 and NIST CSF language in the same control reference.
